## b-limits — beginner

**Question:** What's the difference between the daily loss limit and the maximum loss?

**Tools:** explain_rule, check_budget · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **16.2 s**

> Both are loss ceilings on a funded account, but they're measured over different windows and from different reference points.
> 
> **Formulas**
> 
> ```
> daily_limit  = quota × daily_loss%                (fixed size, from the initial balance)
> daily_floor  = day_start − daily_limit
> daily_budget = equity − daily_floor
> 
> dd_floor     = quota × (1 − max_loss%)             (static — Bitfunded) or
>              = high_water_mark − quota × max_loss% (trailing — other firms)
> dd_budget    = equity − dd_floor
> ```
> 
> `daily_limit` resets every day and measures loss against where the balance stood at the start of *that* day. `max_loss` never resets — it measures loss against the account's starting quota (or, on a trailing product, against the highest balance/equity reached) for the life of the account.
> 
> **Why it matters**
> 
> They bind at different times. A big loss on day one can hit the daily floor long before it gets near the maximum-loss floor; conversely, a string of small losses over many days can creep up on the maximum-loss floor while each day individually stays inside the daily limit. Whichever floor is closer to current equity is the one that actually constrains the next trade — "the binding ceiling."
> 
> **Worked example — Bitfunded's $100,000 1-Step, on troid's reference account**
> 
> At day-start = equity = $100,000:
> 
> - daily floor = $96,000 → daily budget = $4,000 (day_start − quota×4%)
> - max-loss floor = $94,000 → drawdown budget = $6,000 (quota×(1−6%))
> - binding ceiling = the smaller of the two = **daily loss limit, $4,000** of room
> 
> There's also a crossover point: at day-start equity of $98,000 (quota×(1 − 6% + 4%)), the two budgets are equal; below that, the maximum-loss floor is the one that actually governs, and above it, the daily limit governs. This is DERIVED arithmetic, using Bitfunded's own numbers, SOURCED to the help centre's Challenge & Trader Stage table and Terms 9(a) (daily/max %) and the FAQ (daily basis = initial balance), all read as noted above.
> 
> **What it means:** whichever ceiling is nearer to current equity is the one that will actually stop the next trade — not necessarily the one with the smaller headline percentage. Both count floating losses on open positions on Bitfunded (Criteria to be Success, read 2026-09-24): a losing position can fail the account before it's closed.

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **25.7 s**

> The stop is what turns a dollar risk amount into a position size — without it there's nothing to divide by.
> 
> **Formula:** `qty = risk / (|entry − stop| + fee_unit)`, where risk is the dollars a trader is willing to lose (risk% × balance, capped at 35% of the binding budget) and fee_unit is the round-trip fee cost per unit.
> 
> **Why it works:** the distance between entry and stop is the loss per unit if the trade is wrong. To hold a fixed dollar risk constant, position size must shrink as that distance grows, and grow as it shrinks. Leverage doesn't enter this at all — it only sets margin, not risk (see troid's note on leverage and margin). Without a stop, "how much can be lost" has no answer, so there's no way to price a position against a firm's loss ceilings.
> 
> **Worked example**, using a $500 risk and Bitfunded's 1-Step fee:
> 
> At entry 77,872 and stop 76,580, the stop distance is 1,292. Adding the round-trip fee (0.04% per side on Bitfunded's 1-Step, so 62.2976 per unit), quantity comes out to 500 ÷ (1,292 + 62.2976) = 0.369195 units. That gives a notional of 28,749.96 and, at 5× leverage, a margin of 5,749.99.
> 
> Move the stop closer — say to 77,500, a 372 distance — and the same $500 risk buys roughly 3.5 times as many units, with a much larger notional and margin at the same leverage. The stop is what fixes the loss per unit; everything else (quantity, notional, margin) follows from it. The stop, the position and the risk are the trader's; troid only prices them from the numbers given. Leverage never enters the loss calculation at all — it only changes the margin posted, not what's at risk.

## q-expectancy — quantitative

**Question:** My win rate is 40% and my average win is 1.5R against an average loss of 1R. What's my expectancy, and what win rate would I need to break even?

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **12.1 s**

> Expectancy is 0R per trade, and the break-even win rate is 40% — exactly the win rate given.
> 
> **Formula:** E = p × W − (1 − p) × L, where p is win rate, W average win, L average loss. Break-even win rate = L ÷ (W + L) = 1 ÷ (1 + W/L).
> 
> **Why it works:** expectancy is the average result per trade over many trades; setting it to zero and solving for p gives the win rate at which wins and losses exactly cancel, given the payoff ratio.
> 
> **Worked example (yours):** p = 0.4, W = 1.5R, L = 1R → E = 0.4 × 1.5 − 0.6 × 1 = 0.6 − 0.6 = 0R. Payoff ratio W ÷ L = 1.5. Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + 1.5) = 1 ÷ 2.5 = 40%.
> 
> **What it means:** at 40% wins, 1.5R average win and 1R average loss, this system's arithmetic expectancy is exactly zero — the win rate you have is exactly the win rate this payoff ratio needs to break even, before any fees or costs are added. Any win rate below 40% at this same payoff ratio, or any tightening of the payoff ratio below 1.5, would put expectancy below zero.

## q-stats — quantitative

**Question:** Over 78 trades my average is +0.033R with a standard deviation of 0.40R. Is that a real edge?

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **14.1 s**

> No — the 95% confidence interval spans −0.056R to +0.122R, and it contains zero, so this result can't be distinguished from no edge at all.
> 
> **Formula:** standard error = sd ÷ √n; t = mean ÷ SE; 95% CI = mean ± 1.96 × SE.
> 
> **Why it works:** a single average, on its own, doesn't say whether a strategy has an edge or just got lucky. The standard error measures how much that average would bounce around if the same 78-trade run were repeated; the confidence interval shows the range of true means consistent with what was observed. If zero sits inside that range, the data can't rule out "no edge."
> 
> **Worked example (the numbers given):** with mean = +0.033R, sd = 0.40R, n = 78, the standard error is 0.40 ÷ √78 ≈ 0.0453R. That gives t = 0.033 ÷ 0.0453 ≈ 0.729, and a 95% confidence interval of roughly [−0.056R, +0.122R].
> 
> **What it means:** the interval contains zero, so +0.033R over 78 trades is not distinguishable from a zero-edge process at this sample size. This is the same shape of result troid's own strategy shows: out of sample, on data its parameters never saw, it measured +0.008R per trade on BTC (504 trades, standard error 0.016R) and +0.008R on ETH (498 trades, standard error 0.016R), both with 95% intervals containing zero — MEASURED, and not stated as fact. ## q-correlation — quantitative

**Question:** I hold four positions and every pair is correlated at 0.6. How many independent bets is that, roughly?

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **8.5 s**

> Roughly 1.43 independent bets.
> 
> Formula: effective bets = n ÷ (1 + (n − 1) × ρ), where n is the number of positions and ρ the pairwise correlation.
> 
> Why it works: perfectly correlated positions (ρ = 1) move together, so no matter how many you hold, they behave as one bet; uncorrelated positions (ρ = 0) each add a full independent bet. The formula interpolates between those extremes.
> 
> Worked example: with n = 4 and ρ = 0.6, effective bets = 4 ÷ (1 + 3 × 0.6) = 4 ÷ 2.8 = 1.43.
> 
> What it means for your situation: four positions that are all correlated at 0.6 to each other diversify like about 1.43 independent bets, not four — the risk-reduction benefit of holding four rather than one is small, and any loss ceiling that counts floating loss across all open positions together will move almost as one unit if the underlying moves against all four at once.

## p-crossover — prop-rule

**Question:** What is the crossover on a $100,000 Bitfunded 1-Step, and why does it matter?

**Tools:** explain_rule · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **11.3 s**

> The crossover on a $100,000 Bitfunded 1-Step is **$98,000** in day-start balance.
> 
> **Formula:** crossover = quota × (1 − max% + daily%)
> 
> **Why it works:** the maximum-loss floor is a static amount below the starting quota (6% under Bitfunded's 1-Step), while the daily limit is a fixed dollar amount, also set from the initial balance (4%). Below the crossover, the max-loss floor sits closer than the daily limit and is what actually stops the trader first; above it, the daily limit is the closer one. Whichever is closer that day is the one that binds — sizing against the other one is sizing against a ceiling that isn't really there that day.
> 
> **Worked example:** on $100,000 with a 4% daily limit and a 6% maximum loss, crossover = 100,000 × (1 − 0.06 + 0.04) = 100,000 × 0.98 = $98,000.
> 
> **What it means for the account:** $98,000 is only $2,000 below the $100,000 starting balance — about half of one bad day at the 4% daily limit. Once the day-start balance drops below $98,000, the account is really governed by the 6% maximum-loss floor, not the advertised 4% daily limit, even though the daily limit still technically applies. Both ceilings count floating losses on open positions, not only closed ones — an open position that reaches either one fails the account with no close required, per Bitfunded's help centre (Criteria to be Success).
