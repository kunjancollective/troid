**Question:** Can you run a Monte Carlo simulation of my strategy? 55% win rate, 1.2R average win, 1% risk per trade, 100 trades.

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **14.3 s**

> ask troid does not run simulations, including with your inputs here — no new Monte Carlo is computed on the fly. What can be computed through the tools is the closed-form arithmetic: expectancy per trade and the implied total over 100 trades.
> 
> **Answer:** expectancy = 0.21R per trade, for an expected total of 21R over 100 trades — but that is a mean over many runs, not what any one run of 100 trades will produce. troid can't run the dispersion around that mean (the Monte Carlo itself) here.
> 
> **Formula:** `E = p × W − (1 − p) × L`, where p is win rate, W is the average win, L is the average loss.
> 
> **Why it works:** expectancy is the average outcome per trade if the stated win rate and payoff ratio hold exactly; it says nothing about the variance of any particular sequence of trades.
> 
> **Worked example (your numbers):** p = 0.55, W = 1.2R, L = 1R.
> E = 0.55 × 1.2 − 0.45 × 1 = 0.66 − 0.45 = 0.21R per trade.
> Break-even win rate at this payoff ratio (1.2) is 1 ÷ (1.2 + 1) = 45.45% — your 55% is above it.
> Expected total over 100 trades = 100 × 0.21 = 21R.
> 
> **What this means for the situation described:** the 0.21R/trade and 21R/100-trade figures are DERIVED arithmetic from the inputs given, assuming the 55% win rate and 1.2R payoff hold exactly over all 100 trades — an assumption, not a measured fact. troid's own published strategy result is MEASURED, out of sample, at +0.008R per trade on BTC (504 trades) and +0.008R on ETH (498 trades), both with 95% confidence intervals containing zero — i.e., no measurable edge in that sample. troid does not run new Monte Carlo simulations with arbitrary inputs; for the dispersion around an expectancy like this (e.g., probability of being down after 100 trades), that would require a simulation troid cannot generate here. At 1% risk per trade, 21R expected corresponds to roughly 21% of the account in expectancy terms, before accounting for variance, fees, and any firm loss ceiling that would bind well before that.
