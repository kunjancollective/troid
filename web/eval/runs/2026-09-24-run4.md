## ex-r — beginner (a worked example in TROID-CHARACTER.md)

**Question:** What does R mean?

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **16.9 s**

> R is the number that measures a trade's result in units of what was risked, not in dollars alone.
> 
> **Formula:** `1R = |entry − stop| × position size`
> 
> **Why it works:** measuring every trade in R lets results of different sizes, symbols and account values be compared on one common scale — a trade that risks $500 and one that risks $5,000 both read as "−1R" if the stop is hit.
> 
> **In practice**, take a worked example: long BTC at entry 77,872 with a stop at 76,580 and a position of 0.3862 BTC.
> 
> The stop sits 1,292 below entry, so the position's loss if stopped out is 1,292 × 0.3862 ≈ **$498.97** — that is 1R. A trade that closes $1,000 up on that position is about +2R; one stopped out is exactly −1R (before fees; troid's desk also folds in the round-trip fee, which nudges 1R a little higher on a real account).
> 
> **What it means:** whatever dollar figure a trade risks, calling it "1R" lets it be compared directly against any other trade — a $500 loss and a $5,000 loss on a bigger account are both "−1R" if they're each one full stop-out.

## b-limits — beginner

**Question:** What's the difference between the daily loss limit and the maximum loss?

**Tools:** explain_rule · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **13.4 s**

> Two different ceilings, and they bind at different times.
> 
> **The daily loss limit** caps how much the account can lose in a single trading day, reset each day.
> ```
> daily_limit  = quota × daily_loss%
> daily_budget = daily_limit + (equity − day_start)
> ```
> `day_start` is the balance at the last daily reset; today's net profit or loss widens or shrinks the room left, but the limit itself resets fresh each day.
> 
> **The maximum loss** caps the total drawdown from account inception (or, for a trailing product, from the account's high-water mark), with no daily reset.
> ```
> static:   dd_floor = quota × (1 − max_loss%)          [e.g. Bitfunded — measured from the quota, so profit permanently widens the buffer]
> trailing: dd_floor = high_water_mark × (1 − max_loss%) [e.g. BrightFunded — the floor follows the high-water mark up]
> dd_budget = equity − dd_floor
> ```
> 
> **Why it matters:** on any given day, whichever produces the smaller remaining budget is the one that actually governs —
> ```
> effective_budget = min(daily_budget, dd_budget)
> ```
> Early in an account, close to the starting balance, the daily limit is usually the tighter one. But losses accumulate against the max-loss floor without resetting, so after a drawdown the max loss can become the binding ceiling well before the daily limit is touched — and under a static structure like Bitfunded's, both auto-fail on a floating loss, not just a closed one.
> 
> Bitfunded's max loss is SOURCED as static, measured from the account quota (help centre, Criteria to be Success, read 2026-09-18); BrightFunded's trails the equity high-water mark (help centre, read 2026-09-22); Crypto Fund Trader's trailing-on-balance mechanic has no source recorded yet.
> 
> If you give troid a firm, product, quota and equity, troid can compute the exact crossover point where the two ceilings swap which one binds.
>

**Question:** Why does troid need my stop price to size a trade?

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **15.6 s**

> The stop price sets the distance between entry and stop — and that distance is what turns a dollar risk amount into a position size.
> 
> Formula:
> `qty = risk / (|entry − stop| + fee_unit)`
> where risk is the dollar amount troid is willing to put on the trade, and fee_unit is the round-trip fee cost per unit (entry × fee per side × 2).
> 
> Why it matters: the same dollar risk produces a very different position size depending on how far away the stop sits. A tight stop lets a larger position carry the same risk; a wide stop forces a smaller one. Without the stop, "risk $500" has no way to become a quantity of the asset — there's no distance to divide it by.
> 
> In practice, take troid's tools directly:

> 
> What this means: without a stop, there is no distance to divide the risk by, so no quantity, no notional, and no margin figure can be produced at all — the stop is the one input that turns a dollar risk into an actual trade size. It's also what troid uses to check the trade against a firm's daily and maximum loss ceilings, since those too are measured against risk in dollars, not position size directly.
