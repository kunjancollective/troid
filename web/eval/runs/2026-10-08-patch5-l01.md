> Working the expectancy now:
> 
> **The answer:** at 55% win rate, 1.2R average win, 1R average loss (your 1% risk per trade as the unit of R), expectancy per trade is **+0.21R**, and the expected total over 100 trades is **+21R** — but that's a mean over many hypothetical runs, not what any single 100-trade run will actually produce. That single-run variance is exactly what a Monte Carlo simulation would show, and troid doesn't run one here.
> 
> **The formula:**
> `E = p × W − (1 − p) × L`
> `break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + W/L)`
> 
> **Why it works:** expectancy is the probability-weighted average outcome per trade. The break-even win rate tells you the minimum win rate needed at that payoff ratio just to net zero.
> 
> **Worked example (yours):**
> - p = 0.55
> - E = 0.55 × 1.2 − 0.45 × 1 = **0.21R** per trade
> - payoff ratio W/L = 1.2
> - break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + 1.2) = **45.45%**
> - expected total over 100 trades = 100 × 0.21 = **21R**
> 
> **What it means for you:** a 55% win rate clears the 45.45% break-even threshold implied by a 1.2R payoff ratio, by DERIVED arithmetic alone — this says nothing about variance, drawdown path, or the chance of a losing stretch along the way, which is what a Monte Carlo simulation would address. troid does not run new simulations with user-supplied inputs; its only published Monte Carlo figures are in its methodology (MODELLED, with their own stated assumptions — not this win rate/payoff combination) and are not a substitute for this calculation.
