## b-stop — beginner

**Question:** Why does troid need my stop price to size a trade?

**Tools:** trade_math, trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **30.5 s**

> The stop is what turns a dollar risk into a position size — without it there's no way to know how many units of the asset one dollar of loss corresponds to.
> 
> **Formula:** quantity equals the dollar risk divided by the sum of the stop distance and the round-trip fee cost per unit.
> 
> **Why it works:** the loss on a trade is the distance from entry to stop multiplied by position size. Fix the dollar risk you're willing to take, and the stop distance is the only thing left that determines how large the position can be — a tighter stop allows a larger position for the same dollar risk, a wider stop a smaller one.
> 
> **In practice:** risking $500 with an entry at 77,872 and a stop at 76,580, the stop distance is 1,292, and adding the Bitfunded 1-Step's round-trip fee (0.04% per side, so 62.2976 per unit — DERIVED and SOURCED to the help centre's Criteria to be Success, read 2026-09-18 and 2026-09-23) gives a quantity of 0.369195, a notional of 28,749.96.
> 
> Widen the stop instead, to 76,200 — a distance of 1,672 rather than 1,292, with the same fee per unit — and the same $500 of risk buys a smaller position: quantity 0.288301, notional 22,450.59. The dollar risk is unchanged; only the size moved.
> 
> **What it means:** change the stop and the size changes with it, for the same dollar risk. Without the stop, troid can't tell how many units correspond to $500 of loss, so it can't compute quantity, notional, margin, or how much of the account's remaining budget the trade consumes. That's also why a stop given as a percent distance is priced from the entry and the side, rather than a price troid or the user works out by hand — the distance is what the formula runs on.
