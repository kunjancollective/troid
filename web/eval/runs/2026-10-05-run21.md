## b-limits — beginner

**Question:** What's the difference between the daily loss limit and the maximum loss?

**Tools:** check_budget · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **10.9 s**

> Two different ceilings, and only one of them binds at any moment — the smaller one.
> 
> **Formula**
> daily budget = equity − (day_start − quota × daily%)
> drawdown budget = equity − quota × (1 − max%)
> binding = min(daily budget, drawdown budget)
> 
> **Why it works:** the daily limit resets every day from a fixed amount off the day's starting balance; the maximum loss is a floor set once, off the account's starting quota (static on Bitfunded), that never resets. On a fresh day they're both live, but they move differently as the day goes on, so whichever is smaller controls what the account can actually lose before it fails.
> 
> **Worked example** — troid's reference account, a $100,000 Bitfunded 1-Step, fresh day, equity at quota:
> 
> - daily floor = day_start − quota × 4% = 100,000 − 4,000 = 96,000 → daily budget = equity − 96,000 = $4,000
> - max-loss floor = quota × (1 − 6%) = 94,000 → drawdown budget = equity − 94,000 = $6,000
> - binding = min(4,000, 6,000) = **daily loss limit, $4,000**
> 
> **What it means for this account:** at the start of a fresh day the daily limit is the tighter ceiling ($4,000 of room vs $6,000), so it's the one that actually fails the account first. The two cross at equity = $98,000 (quota × (1 − 6% + 4%)): below that day-start balance the max-loss floor becomes the tighter one instead, and above it (including the $100,000 start) the daily limit binds. Both count floating losses on open positions, not just closed ones — Bitfunded fails the account automatically once an open position reaches either limit.

## b-leverage — beginner

**Question:** If I use 10x leverage instead of 2x, do I lose more when my stop is hit?

**Tools:** trade_math, trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **17.2 s**

> Not necessarily — it depends on whether the margin fits, not on leverage itself.
> 
> Formula: loss at stop = quantity × (|entry − stop| + fee per unit), where quantity = risk ÷ (|entry − stop| + fee per unit), unless the margin that size requires exceeds equity — then quantity is cut to equity × leverage ÷ entry, and the loss is less than the intended risk.
> 
> Why: leverage only sets how much margin is posted for a given size (margin = notional ÷ leverage); it doesn't appear in the risk formula itself. The stop distance and the quantity set the loss.
> 
> Worked example (no firm rule, numbers chosen for the illustration): entry 50,000, stop 2% away at 49,000, intended risk $500, equity $10,000.
> 
> - At 10× leverage: quantity = 500 ÷ 1,000 = 0.5, notional = $25,000, margin = 25,000 ÷ 10 = $2,500 — fits in the $10,000 equity, so the loss at the stop is the full intended $500.
> - At 2× leverage: the same risk-based quantity (0.5) would need notional $25,000 and margin 25,000 ÷ 2 = $12,500 — more than the $10,000 equity, so the size is cut to equity × leverage ÷ entry = 10,000 × 2 ÷ 50,000 = 0.4. That gives notional $20,000, margin $10,000, and a loss at the stop of 0.4 × 1,000 = $400 — less than the intended $500.
> 
> What it means: in this example the lower leverage (2×) is the one that produces the smaller loss, because it's the one whose margin didn't fit the full risk-based size and got cut down. > 
> **Expectancy**
> 
> Formula: `E = p × W − (1 − p) × L`, where p is the win rate, W the average win in R, L the average loss in R.
> 
> Why it works: expectancy is the average result per trade if the win rate and payoff hold over many trades — it weights each outcome by how often it happens.
> 
> Worked example: p = 0.55, W = 1.2, L = 1 → E = 0.55 × 1.2 − 0.45 × 1 = **0.21R per trade**. > 
> **Kelly, for context**
> 
> Formula: `f* = p − (1 − p) ÷ b`, where b = W ÷ L, the payoff ratio.
> 
> Why it works: it is the fraction of equity per trade that maximizes long-run growth, if p and b are known exactly — which they never are.
