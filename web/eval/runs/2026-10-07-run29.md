> R is the amount risked on one trade — the loss if the stop is hit, used as a unit to measure every trade on the same scale.
>

A trade stopped out at 76,580 loses this full amount: −1R, or −$522.83.

## b-limits — beginner

**Question:** What's the difference between the daily loss limit and the maximum loss?

**Tools:** explain_rule, check_budget · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **13.5 s**

> The daily loss limit and the maximum loss are two separate ceilings, and whichever is smaller at a given moment is the one that actually constrains the account — the other is not currently binding.
> 
> **The daily loss limit** is a fixed amount set once per challenge, measured from the account's starting balance. On Bitfunded's product rules, it's a fixed 4% of the initial quota, reset each day. The floor it sets moves each day: `daily_floor = day_start − quota × 4%`, and `daily_budget = equity − daily_floor`.
> 
> **The maximum loss** is measured from the account's starting quota too (on Bitfunded, which uses a static floor — some other firms trail it off a high-water mark instead). It doesn't reset: `dd_floor = quota × (1 − 6%)`, and `dd_budget = equity − dd_floor`.
> 
> Worked example, on troid's reference account — a $100,000 Bitfunded 1-Step, fresh day, equity at $100,000:
> 
> - daily floor = $100,000 − $100,000 × 4% = $96,000 → daily budget = $100,000 − $96,000 = **$4,000**
> - max-loss floor = $100,000 × (1 − 6%) = $94,000 → drawdown budget = $100,000 − $94,000 = **$6,000**
> - binding = min($4,000, $6,000) = the **daily loss limit**
> 
> What this means: at the start of a day, the daily limit is the tighter ceiling ($4,000 of room vs $6,000), so it binds. The two swap when the day starts low enough — on this product, below a day-start equity of $98,000 the maximum-loss floor becomes the tighter one and binds instead, since it no longer moves with the day but the daily floor keeps chasing the day's start down. Both count floating losses on open positions, not just closed ones, and Bitfunded auto-fails the account the moment either is reached — no need to close the position first.

## b-stop — beginner

**Question:** Why does troid need my stop price to size a trade?

**Tools:** trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **19.0 s**

> Because the stop sets the size — not the other way around.
> 
> Formula: quantity = risk ÷ (|entry − stop| + fee per unit), where risk is the dollar amount being risked, |entry − stop| is the stop distance, and fee per unit is the entry and exit fee charged on that unit.
> 
> Worked example, risking $500 on the trade above (Bitfunded 1-Step, 0.04% fee per side):
> 
> - stop distance = |entry − stop| = 1,292
> - fee per unit = (entry + stop) × 0.04% = $61.78
> - quantity = risk ÷ (stop distance + fee per unit) = 500 ÷ (1,292 + 61.78) = 0.369336
> - notional = quantity × entry = $28,760.93
> - fees = quantity × fee per unit = $22.82
> - loss at the stop = quantity × (stop distance + fee per unit) = $500, the risk itself, fees included
> 
> Without the stop, troid has no distance to divide the risk by, so it can't tell how many units of BTC turn a move into $500 of loss. A tighter stop at the same risk gives a larger quantity; a wider stop gives a smaller one. The stop is the trader's; troid prices what it implies, not what it should be.
>

## b-leverage — beginner

**Question:** If I use 10x leverage instead of 2x, do I lose more when my stop is hit?

**Tools:** trade_math, trade_math · **model:** claude-sonnet-5 · **24.2 s**

> No — the loss at the stop is the same at 10x as at 2x, as long as the margin required fits inside the account's equity.
> 
> Formula: `risk = |entry − stop| × quantity`, and `quantity = risk ÷ (|entry − stop| + fee per unit)`. Leverage doesn't appear in either — quantity is set by how much you choose to risk and how far away the stop is. Leverage only sets `margin = notional ÷ leverage`, where `notional = quantity × entry`. Why it works this way: the stop defines the loss per unit held, so once quantity is fixed, the loss is fixed; leverage only determines how much of that notional is posted as margin versus borrowed, which changes what's tied up and where exchange liquidation sits, not what's lost at the stop. The one exception: if the margin the risk-based size requires is larger than the account's equity, the size has to be cut to `quantity = equity × leverage ÷ entry`, and in that case a lower leverage does produce a smaller loss, because it forces a smaller position.
> 
> Worked example, long at entry 77,872, stop 74,814, risking $500 on $100,000 equity:
> 
> - At 10x: stop distance = |77,872 − 74,814| = 3,058; quantity = 500 ÷ 3,058 = 0.163506; notional = 0.163506 × 77,872 = $12,732.50; margin = 12,732.50 ÷ 10 = $1,273.25
> - At 2x: same stop distance, same quantity 0.163506, same notional $12,732.50; margin = 12,732.50 ÷ 2 = $6,366.25
> 
> The loss at the stop is $500 either way — identical. Only the margin changed: $1,273.25 vs $6,366.25, both well inside the $100,000 equity, so neither hits the margin-exceeds-equity exception.
> 
> For you: the leverage choice here only affects how much margin is tied up and, under isolated margin, how far price can move against the position before exchange liquidation — not what is lost if the stop is hit. The only way raising leverage would shrink this trade is if the margin at the lower leverage exceeded equity, forcing a cut — it doesn't, at either leverage, in this example.
